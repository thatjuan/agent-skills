## Line by line

### Situation

**Test:** within seconds the viewer knows where we are, who matters, and what normal looks like here. It is a specific world rather than "a busy professional" or "modern life."

- **The opening is a promise** (Andrew Stanton). The first image tells the audience what kind of story this is and that it will be worth their time. Make it raise a question.
- **Opening image, final image** (Blake Snyder). Design the first frame so the last frame can mirror it and show the change.
- **Save the cat** (Snyder). Early on, show the character doing something that makes us like them. In a 30s spot this happens in the first 3–5 seconds.
- **"Once upon a time… Every day…"** (Pixar Story Spine). The Situation is the routine the story is about to break.

### Desire

**Test:** the want is something we can watch the character pursue, and the audience can feel it, ideally because they want it too. That shared want is where viewers lean in and start caring whether the character gets it.

- **Conscious desire, unconscious need** (Robert McKee). The character chases one thing and is missing another. The strongest Results pay off both.
- **Make me care** (Stanton). Desire is what turns a viewer into an investor in the outcome.
- **Active characters** (Pixar #13). Give the character opinions and a will; passive heroes kill a story.
- **Customer language** (Donald Miller, StoryBrand). In an ad, state the desire the way the customer would say it, never as a product feature.
- **Trying beats succeeding** (Pixar #1). We admire characters for the attempt.

### Conflict

**Test:** if the obstacle vanished, the story would end. Name the cost of failure; if nothing is lost, there is no Conflict.

- **Story is conflict** (McKee). Without tension there is no reason to keep watching.
- **The gap** (McKee). Drama lives in the gap between what the character expects to happen and what does. Open it early; the inciting incident lands by second 3 of a 30s spot.
- **Three levels** (Miller). External (the practical obstacle), internal (how it makes them feel), philosophical (why it is wrong that anyone should face it). A spot rarely needs all three, but naming them sharpens the one you use.
- **Throw the opposite** (Pixar #6). Find what the character is good at or comfortable with, then hit them with its opposite.
- **Conflict is more interesting** (Luke Sullivan). A setup without tension is wallpaper.
- **Inner obstacles count.** Self-doubt, fear, and excuses are real Conflict, especially in creator and founder stories.

### Change

**Test:** the shift comes from the character's choice, action, or earned insight. It can be big or small; a new way of seeing counts. Coincidence that gets a character out of trouble is cheating (Pixar #19).

- **The guide enables, the hero acts** (Miller). In an ad the brand offers the plan or the tool, but the customer makes the move.
- **Show the turn.** A realization on screen is a gesture: a hand stops mid-reach, a long look, a door opened. "She realizes" is not filmable.
- **Unexpected** (Chip and Dan Heath). The Change often carries the surprise, the reversal, or the demonstration that earns attention.
- **Movement forward.** The audience should feel the story move; it is where growth becomes visible.

### Result

**Test:** the new reality visibly differs from the Situation and ties the story together. A viewer leaves with closure and one clear takeaway.

- **Mirror the opening** (Snyder). Return to the opening image, changed.
- **Moment of reflection** (Ira Glass). In creator, documentary, and founder work, follow the anecdote with a line that says what it meant.
- **Earn the end frame** (Sullivan). The tagline pays off the setup instead of labeling it.
- **End on the product** (David Ogilvy). In ads, spots that close on the pack shift brand preference more than spots that don't. Integrate it into the Result.

## Worked examples

### Ad: Riverside Coffee, 30s

- **Situation:** At 4:47 a.m., Maya, a new mother in a one-bedroom city apartment, rocks her newborn back to sleep.
- **Desire:** She wants one small thing for herself: a real coffee.
- **Conflict:** Her arms are full, the kitchen is too far, and the baby wakes at the slightest sound.
- **Change:** One thumb-tap on a saved order, and across the dark city a cyclist and a barista start moving for her.
- **Result:** She opens the door to a steaming cup with a marker sun on the lid, the baby asleep on her chest, and takes the first sip of a morning that is hers.

### Creator video: a first YouTube video

- **Situation:** I'm a filmmaker from the Netherlands who has loved making videos since I was a kid.
- **Desire:** My dream was to be a YouTuber, sharing stories from the heart instead of only making work for clients.
- **Conflict:** Every time I tried to start, self-doubt and overthinking pulled me back.
- **Change:** When I let go of the YouTube dream, I saw I'd been focusing on the wrong reasons; I just wanted to create stories.
- **Result:** So I finally sat down and started. This video is the result, and that first step pushed me to go all in.

## Expanding the lines

The five lines are a launchpad. Grow them into ten, twenty, and then the full script; each pass adds detail and texture, and none changes the core. The same creator story at ten lines:

1. I'm a filmmaker from the Netherlands and I've loved making videos since I was a kid. *(Situation)*
2. My dream was to be a YouTuber, sharing stories from the heart, not just for clients. *(Desire)*
3. But every time I tried to start, self-doubt and overthinking pulled me back. *(Conflict)*
4. My mind kept yelling that you can't go from ads for big companies to YouTube videos. *(Conflict)*
5. The fears became excuses: too late, too old, not enough skill, not the right gear, no security. *(Conflict)*
6. So I let go of the dream, and at first it felt good. I felt free. *(Change)*
7. But one question kept coming back: why did I want to do YouTube at all? *(Change)*
8. Stripped of views, subscribers, and money, it was about the joy of creating and sharing stories. *(Change)*
9. That pushed me through the doubt and onto the process, and this video is the result. *(Result)*
10. It wasn't easy, but I've learned a lot, and now I'm going all in and sharing every lesson. *(Result)*

Rules for every expansion:

- **Tag each new line** with the five-line line it serves. A line that serves none is decoration; cut it.
- **But and therefore** (Trey Parker and Matt Stone). Consecutive beats connect by *but* (a complication) or *therefore* (a consequence). *And then* means the beats are a list, not a story.
- **Proportion follows runtime.** Conflict and Change usually earn the most lines; Situation earns the fewest in short formats.
- **Come back when lost.** When a project sprawls in production or the edit, reread the five lines and check each scene, shot, and transition against them. Whatever cannot connect to a line is a candidate to cut.

## Other frameworks inside the five lines

Every major framework is a finer grid over the same five lines. Use one to shape beats inside the lines when the format calls for it, never to replace them.

| Framework | Situation | Desire | Conflict | Change | Result | Best for |
|-----------|-----------|--------|----------|--------|--------|----------|
| **Pixar Story Spine** (Emma Coats) | Once upon a time… Every day… | (implied in the routine) | One day… | Because of that… because of that… | Until finally… | 15–30s, simple emotional arcs |
| **StoryBrand SB7** (Miller) | A character | wants something | has a problem; failure at stake | meets a guide, gets a plan, is called to act | ends in success | Brand films, explainers, DTC |
| **Hero's Journey** (Campbell, Vogler) | Ordinary world | Call to adventure | Refusal, tests, ordeal | Mentor, threshold, reward | Return with the elixir | 60s+ transformation films |
| **Save the Cat** (Snyder) | Opening image, setup | Theme stated, catalyst | Debate, bad guys close in, all is lost | Break into three | Finale, final image | Character-driven narrative spots |
| **Problem, Agitation, Solution** | Problem | (relief from it) | Agitation | Solution | (life after) | Direct response, performance |
| **Two worlds** (Sullivan) | World A | World B glimpsed | The gap between them | Crossing over | World B lived | Comparison, repositioning |
| **Anecdote and reflection** (Glass) | Setup of the anecdote | What the teller wanted | What went wrong | The moment of insight | Reflection: what it meant | Creator, documentary, founder |

Annette Simmons's six story types (who I am, why I'm here, vision, teaching, values in action, I know what you're thinking) are useful for founder and B2B work: choose the type first, then write its five lines.

## Stickiness

After the five lines pass, run the premise through the Heaths' SUCCESs checklist and strengthen the weakest:

- **Simple:** one core idea; strip everything that isn't it.
- **Unexpected:** break a pattern early.
- **Concrete:** sensory and filmable ("a gorilla drumming to Phil Collins," not "joy").
- **Credible:** a demonstration, a real person, a detail only an insider would know.
- **Emotional:** feeling for one person, not for an abstraction.
- **Story:** satisfied by the five lines.
