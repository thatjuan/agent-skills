# Canon

What the books on advertising and creativity teach, condensed to principles you can apply. Each entry gives the source, the principle, and the question it puts to a brief. Masters disagree (Reeves's hard sell against Bernbach's soft persuasion, Hopkins's testing against Feldwick's showmanship), and that disagreement is useful: each angle in step 3 can come from a different school.

## Method

### James Webb Young: *A Technique for Producing Ideas*
- **Principle:** An idea is a new combination of old elements. The talent for combining is the habit of seeing relationships between facts.
- **The five steps:** (1) gather raw material, both specific (product, audience) and general (life, culture); (2) digest it, turning facts over and looking for how they fit together; (3) drop the problem and let the unconscious work; (4) the idea arrives, usually when you are not looking for it; (5) shape the idea and expose it to criticism until it fits real conditions.
- **Ask:** Do I have enough raw material for a combination to happen? What two facts on this sheet have never been put side by side?

### Isaac Asimov: *On Creativity* (essay)
- **Principle:** New ideas come from people who connect things others had not thought to connect. Generating ideas needs freedom from judgment; judging needs a separate pass.
- **Ask:** Am I judging while generating? Which two distant fields does this brief sit between?

### Ed Catmull: *Creativity, Inc.*
- **Principle:** Early ideas are "ugly babies": awkward, unfinished, easily killed. Protect them long enough to develop, then subject them to candid review (Pixar's Braintrust).
- **Ask:** Is the promising-but-ugly idea being cut because it is wrong, or because it is unfinished?

## The hard sell: facts, claims, testing

### Claude C. Hopkins: *Scientific Advertising*
- **Principle:** Advertising is salesmanship in print. Specific facts sell; platitudes slide off. Test everything that can be tested, and use samples and coupons to make response measurable.
- **The preemptive claim:** Schlitz advertised steam-cleaned bottles and water from deep wells. Every brewer did the same, but the first to say it owned it.
- **Ask:** What does this product do that everyone in the category does but nobody says? What is the number, the process, the detail?

### Rosser Reeves: *Reality in Advertising*
- **Principle:** The Unique Selling Proposition has three parts: a specific benefit ("buy this, get this"), one competitors cannot or do not offer, and strong enough to move large numbers of people. Repeat it relentlessly.
- **Example:** M&M's, "Melts in your mouth, not in your hand."
- **Ask:** What is the one benefit only we can claim? Could it be said in seven words?

### Al Ries & Jack Trout: *Positioning*
- **Principle:** The battle is in the prospect's mind, not the product. Be first in a category, or create a new category you can be first in. Own one word (Volvo owns *safety*). Position against the leader when you cannot be the leader.
- **Examples:** 7Up as "the Uncola"; Avis as No. 2.
- **Ask:** What word do we want to own? What is the ladder in the customer's mind, and which rung can we take?

## The creative revolution: craft, honesty, theatre

### David Ogilvy: *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, *Ogilvy on Advertising*
- **Principle:** "We sell, or else." Research the product until you find the fact. Five times as many people read the headline as the body, so the headline carries the sale. Long copy sells when it is interesting. Give the brand a consistent personality (brand image) and build it over years.
- **Story appeal:** The eye patch on the Hathaway shirt man made readers ask who he was.
- **The big idea test:** Did it make me gasp when I first saw it? Do I wish I had thought of it myself? Is it unique? Does it fit the strategy to perfection? Could it be used for 30 years?
- **Ask:** What fact did the research turn up that nobody has used? What would make a reader stop and wonder?

### Bill Bernbach (*Bill's Book*, Bob Levenson; *Think Small*, Dominik Imseng)
- **Principle:** Persuasion is an art, not a science. Honesty is disarming: admit the flaw and the audience believes the rest. Simplicity, wit, and respect for the reader's intelligence. Copywriter and art director work as one team, so word and picture make a single idea.
- **Examples:** Volkswagen "Think Small" and "Lemon"; Avis "We Try Harder."
- **Ask:** What is the product's weakness, and how is it a strength? What would we say if we told the plain truth?

### Leo Burnett
- **Principle:** Find the **inherent drama**: the reason people buy, already present in the product. Warm, folksy, archetypal characters (the Jolly Green Giant, Tony the Tiger, the Marlboro cowboy).
- **Example:** Green Giant peas, "Harvested in the moonlight": a production fact made romantic.
- **Ask:** Where is the drama hidden inside the product itself?

### George Lois: *George, Be Careful*
- **Principle:** The big idea is a single, shocking, simple image-and-word combination that makes people talk. Art direction is a form of argument.
- **Examples:** Esquire covers (Muhammad Ali as Saint Sebastian); "I want my MTV."
- **Ask:** What is the image that would cause an argument at dinner?

### Mary Wells Lawrence: *A Big Life in Advertising*
- **Principle:** Advertising as theatre and as a change to the product itself. She had Braniff's planes painted in bright colors ("the end of the plain plane") rather than only advertising them.
- **Ask:** What could we change in the product, packaging, or experience so that the change is the ad?

### Jerry Della Femina, Phil Dusenberry, Pat Fallon (memoirs)
- **Principle:** The work comes from irreverence, from obsession with the craft, and from treating creativity as leverage: Fallon built a practice on outsized results from smaller budgets.
- **Ask:** If we had a tenth of the budget, what idea would we need?

## Modern strategy: reframing, behavioral science, fame

### Dave Trott: *Predatory Thinking*
- **Principle:** Get upstream of the problem and reframe it. You do not have to outrun the lion, only the other man. Most advertising is invisible, so the first job is to be noticed, the second to be remembered, the third to persuade.
- **Ask:** What is the real problem behind the stated one? What would make this impossible to ignore?

### Rory Sutherland: *Alchemy*
- **Principle:** Logic is a poor guide to human behavior, and the opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea. Changing perception is often cheaper than changing reality. Costly signals create trust.
- **Examples:** Rather than spend billions to make the Eurostar journey shorter, make it so pleasant (supermodels serving free champagne) that passengers would ask for it to be longer. Red Bull's small can, high price, and odd taste signal potency.
- **Ask:** What would we do if this were a psychological problem? What is the opposite of the obvious answer?

### Paul Feldwick: *The Anatomy of Humbug*, *Why Does the Pedlar Sing?*
- **Principle:** The rational models (USP, AIDA) explain less than they claim. Much effective advertising works through **fame** and **showmanship**: entertainment, spectacle, and likability that make a brand famous. The pedlar sings to draw a crowd.
- **Example:** Cadbury Smash's Martians laughing at humans who peel potatoes.
- **Ask:** Would people seek this out? Would they sing it, quote it, retell it?

### James Hurman: *The Case for Creativity* (after Binet & Field)
- **Principle:** Award-winning creative work is measurably more effective. Emotional campaigns outperform rational ones over the long term; fame campaigns perform best of all; split investment between long-term brand building and short-term activation (roughly 60:40).
- **Ask:** Is this idea emotional enough, and famous enough, to build the brand beyond this quarter?

### Luke Sullivan: *Hey, Whipple, Squeeze This*
- **Principle:** Mr. Whipple, who badgered shoppers not to squeeze the Charmin, stands for the advertising everyone hates: loud, repetitive, insulting. Find the tension in the brand. Write hundreds of headlines. Express the strategy as simply as possible, then dramatize it. Your first ideas are everyone's first ideas.
- **Ask:** Would I put this on my wall, or am I settling?

### Bob Schmetterer: *Leap*
- **Principle:** The best creative ideas go beyond advertising and change the business: a new product, service, channel, or behavior ("creative business ideas").
- **Ask:** What if the idea were not an ad at all?

## Persuasion and spread

### Robert Cialdini: *Influence*
Reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity (and later, unity). Every promotion uses at least one; the best use it as the idea rather than as a footnote.

### Chip & Dan Heath: *Made to Stick*
Sticky ideas are **SUCCESs**: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. Use it to sharpen an idea once found: which of the six is it missing?

### Jonah Berger: *Contagious*
Ideas spread through **STEPPS**: Social currency (it makes the sharer look good), Triggers (the environment reminds people), Emotion (high arousal), Public (visible use), Practical value, Stories (the brand rides inside a narrative). Use it to pressure-test a stunt or social idea.

### Daniel Kahneman: *Thinking, Fast and Slow*
Most ad processing is System 1: fast, intuitive, low attention. Framing, loss aversion, anchoring, availability, and the peak-end rule shape choices more than arguments do.

### Dan Ariely: *Predictably Irrational*
People judge by relativity, so a decoy option shifts choice (*The Economist*'s print-only offer priced the same as print-plus-web). FREE is not just a low price; it changes behavior.

### Seth Godin: *Purple Cow*
A brown cow is invisible; a purple cow is remarkable. Being very good is the new boring; safe is risky.

### Malcolm Gladwell: *The Tipping Point*
Connectors, mavens, and salesmen carry ideas; stickiness matters; small changes in context tip behavior.

## History and pattern libraries

Mark Tungate's *Adland*, Kenneth Roman's *The King of Madison Avenue*, Karen Stabiner's *Inventing Desire*, Bart Cummings's *Advertising's Benevolent Dictators*, and the memoirs of Charlie Brower, John Orr Young, Jerry Goodis, and James Webb Young give the case history that [Cases](cases.md) condenses. Mario Pricken's *Creative Advertising* organizes hundreds of campaigns by the creative method behind them, the approach [Lenses](lenses.md) follows.
